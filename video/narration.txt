TriageLens demo narration (about 2 minutes 20 seconds)

[0:00 - 0:15]
Hi, I built TriageLens for the Teens in AI Global Challenge 2027. My question: when a model ranks people for help, who does it miss? It answers in your browser, with no data leaving your device.

[0:15 - 0:40]
I start on the clinic triage scenario. Every row is synthetic, seeded and generated as the page loads. No patient data, no real pilot. I train two models from scratch: an L2-regularized logistic regression and a depth-limited Gini decision tree. You see the real loss curve falling during gradient descent and the feature weights behind each prediction.

[0:40 - 1:05]
Now I raise the historical label bias slider and watch the fairness gap card. The recorded label penalizes rural patients, so the model learns to under-triage them. On held-out rows, the equal-opportunity gap opens to roughly twenty-five percentage points. The subgroup chart shows why: true positives fall for the underserved group while false negatives climb.

[1:05 - 1:35]
Here is the key part. I compare four policies on the same held-out rows. The global threshold is fast but unfair. Equal opportunity sets group-specific thresholds fitted only on training data, cutting the gap from about twenty-five points to under three. The accuracy cost appears in the table, so nothing is hidden. Selection-rate mitigation and proxy removal give two more trade-off points.

[1:35 - 1:55]
I open the counterexample inspector. These are held-out cases where the global threshold said no support but a mitigation said yes. Each card shows the feature vector and the exact threshold that changed the outcome. That is where a fairness metric becomes a human question.

[1:55 - 2:15]
Finally, the ethics panel states who benefits, who could be harmed, likely failure modes, and why a human must decide. TriageLens is a teaching instrument, not a clinical tool. It has zero dependencies and works fully offline. Fairness is not a checkbox. It is a measurement you keep making.
